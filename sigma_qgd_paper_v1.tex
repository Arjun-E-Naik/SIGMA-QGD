\begin{abstract}
Classical optimization remains the main bottleneck in Variational Quantum Eigensolvers (VQEs). Even with exact parameter-shift gradients, quantum energy landscapes present difficult geometry, alternating between narrow, ill-conditioned ravines and broad, nearly flat plateaus. Current optimizers either ignore curvature entirely (such as Adam and SPSA) or compute the Quantum Fisher Information metric at heavy circuit overhead ($3p+2$ circuits per step for diagonal QNG). Here, we introduce \textbf{SIGMA-QGD}. The algorithm builds on a direct physical intuition: the temporal variance of a parameter's gradient component over past steps serves as a zero-extra-circuit proxy for local landscape curvature. We establish an assumption-qualified theoretical foundation for this approach: (1) an asymptotic proportionality between trajectory variance and squared Hessian diagonal elements under local quadraticity and aggregate diagonal dominance ($\delta \sqrt{p} \ll |H_{ii}|$); (2) uniform two-sided bounds on the resulting preconditioner; (3) a descent-direction guarantee under an empirical coordinate-wise descent margin condition; and (4) an ergodic $\mathcal{O}(1/T)$ non-convex stationarity convergence rate ($\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})$ under finite-shot quantum measurement noise). We test this proxy against exact parameter-shift Hessian diagonals across 10- and 12-qubit TFIM and XXZ models, observing strong agreement (Pearson $r \in [0.57, 0.63]$, $r^2 \approx 0.32\text{--}0.39$ on TFIM; $r \approx 0.30$ on XXZ; $p < 10^{-4}$ across $>394{,}000$ coordinate evaluations). Benchmark tests across 11 optimizers on 10- and 12-qubit TFIM and XXZ spin chains show that SIGMA-QGD delivers an order-of-magnitude accuracy lead on commuting TFIM landscapes and secures the lowest mean energy gap on the frustrated XXZ chain within a standard $2p+1$ circuit budget, statistically tying with natural gradient and adaptive baselines on 12-qubit XXZ.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE), Classical Optimization, Curvature Preconditioning, Gradient Trajectory Variance, Quantum Natural Gradient.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Variational Quantum Eigensolvers (VQEs) \cite{peruzzo2014variational,mcclean2016theory} serve as a central tool in noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) computing. In a VQE, a parameterized quantum circuit prepares a quantum state $|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$ with parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^p$. A quantum processor evaluates the expectation value of a target problem Hamiltonian $H$:
\begin{equation}
E(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \langle \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) | H | \psi(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \rangle,
\label{eq:vqe_cost}
\end{equation}
while an external classical optimization routine iteratively updates $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ to estimate the ground-state energy $E_0 = \min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} E(\boldsymbol{\theta})$.

\subsection{The Optimization Bottleneck in VQE}
Exact parameter-shift rules \cite{mitarai2018quantum,schuld2019evaluating} yield unbiased analytic gradients on quantum hardware. Even with exact gradients, however, minimizing $E(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is difficult in practice. Parameterized quantum landscapes present sharply varying curvature \cite{mcclean2018barren,cerezo2021cost}: flat, gradient-starved plateaus sit alongside narrow, highly anisotropic curved valleys \cite{mcclean2018barren,cerezo2021cost,wierichs2020avoiding}.

Standard first-order optimizers struggle in these settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Isotropic and Empirical Variance Optimizers (Adam, RMSProp, Yogi):} Classical adaptive gradient methods \cite{kingma2014adam,tieleman2012lecture,zaheer2018adaptive} track squared gradients or momentum-referenced variance \cite{zhuang2020adabelief}. They assume gradient noise is stationary and uniform across iterations. In quantum landscapes, this assumption fails, causing updates to stall on plateaus or overshoot narrow valleys.
    \item \textbf{Quantum Natural Gradient (QNG):} Metric-aware algorithms \cite{stokes2020quantum,wierichs2020avoiding} rescale gradients via the Fubini--Study Quantum Fisher Information (QFI) metric tensor. This metric helps navigate circuit geometry. However, diagonal QNG requires $(p+1)$ extra circuit evaluations per step, raising the per-step cost from $2p+1$ to $3p+2$ circuits.
    \item \textbf{Derivative-Free Methods (SPSA, COBYLA):} Methods that avoid parameter shifts \cite{spall1992multivariate,novak2025benchmarking} discard curvature information completely, leading to slow convergence in anisotropic landscapes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Trajectory-Variance Curvature Preconditioning}
We ask a basic question: can an optimizer recover meaningful curvature awareness \emph{purely from the temporal statistics of standard parameter-shift gradients}, using \textbf{zero extra quantum circuits}?

We introduce \textbf{SIGMA-QGD} (Sampled Iterative Gradient Moment Adaptation for Quantum Gradient Descent). SIGMA-QGD does not query quantum hardware for quantum state overlaps or second derivatives. Instead, it tracks an online Bessel-corrected Welford accumulator for each parameter coordinate along the trajectory. When a parameter coordinate fluctuates rapidly from step to step, this movement indicates sharp local curvature, signaling that updates along that coordinate require damping. When a coordinate produces steady, low-variance gradients, the optimizer accelerates updates across flat terrain.

\subsection{Theoretical Contributions}
The main contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Theoretical Foundation (Section~\ref{sec:theory}):} We link temporal gradient variance $\hat{\sigma}_i^2$ to local Hessian diagonal curvature $H_{ii}^2$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:variance_curvature}), prove uniform two-sided bounds on the preconditioner (Proposition~\ref{prop:boundedness}), establish a strict descent guarantee under an empirical coordinate-wise margin condition with dynamical self-damping (Proposition~\ref{prop:descent}), and prove $\mathcal{O}(1/T)$ noiseless and $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})$ finite-shot non-convex stationarity convergence rates (Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence}).
    \item \textbf{Empirical Curvature Validation (Section~\ref{sec:exp_proxy}):} We test Proposition~\ref{prop:variance_curvature} directly, measuring Pearson and Spearman correlations between our online variance estimator and the exact parameter-shift diagonal Hessian.
    \item \textbf{Systematic Experimental Protocol (Section~\ref{sec:setup}):} We execute a benchmark protocol that includes an independent learning-rate grid sweep, component ablations, finite-shot noise tests, and budget-matched comparisons against metaheuristics.
    \item \textbf{Benchmark Results (Section~\ref{sec:results}):} We benchmark 11 optimizers (SIGMA-QGD alongside 10 baselines) on 10-qubit and 12-qubit TFIM and XXZ spin chains.
\end{itemize}
All implementation source code, configuration files, raw trajectory data, and plotting pipelines are available at \url{https://github.com/Arjun-E-Naik/SIGMA-QGD}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Adaptive First-Order Optimizers}
First-order adaptive optimizers scale coordinate learning rates using past gradient history. Adam \cite{kingma2014adam} normalizes steps with an exponential moving average of squared uncentered gradients $\mathbb{E}[g_i^2]$. RMSProp \cite{tieleman2012lecture} uses uncentered second moments, while Yogi \cite{zaheer2018adaptive} bounds the growth rate of second-moment updates in non-stationary settings. AdaBelief \cite{zhuang2020adabelief} modifies Adam by computing gradient variance relative to the momentum estimate $\mathbb{E}[(g_i - m_i)^2]$. Unlike these methods, SIGMA-QGD computes the variance around the gradient's own running mean via Welford's algorithm, avoiding conflation with the lagged momentum signal.

\subsection{Quantum Natural Gradient and Curvature Preconditioning}
Quantum Natural Gradient (QNG) \cite{stokes2020quantum} updates parameters according to $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_t - \eta G^{-1} \mathbf{g}_t$, where $G$ is the Fubini--Study metric tensor. While block-diagonal and full QNG require $\mathcal{O}(p^2)$ circuits, diagonal QNG requires $p+1$ metric circuits per iteration. Hamiltonian-aware QNG \cite{shi2025hamiltonian} and Stein-identity QFI estimation \cite{halla2025stein} approximate curvature using specialized circuit evaluations. 

Recent methods such as Hamiltonian-aware QNG (H-QNG)~\cite{shi2025hamiltonian} and Stein-identity QFI estimation~\cite{halla2025stein} reduce circuit overhead compared to full QNG. Still, they differ from SIGMA-QGD in both algorithmic mechanics and scaling. H-QNG constructs a pullback metric tensor from individual Pauli-term derivatives without extra quantum circuits. However, it requires storing and inverting a $p \times p$ (or block-diagonal) Jacobian across individual Hamiltonian sub-terms at each step. In addition, relying on term-by-term derivative access makes H-QNG vulnerable to finite-shot noise when non-commuting Pauli strings are sampled. Stein-identity QFI estimation evaluates the metric tensor via random parameter perturbations, which introduces Monte Carlo sampling noise and step-size hyperparameters like those in 2-SPSA and QN-SPSA~\cite{gacon2021simultaneous}. By comparison, SIGMA-QGD operates purely on the total energy gradient vector $\mathbf{g}_t$ in $\mathcal{O}(p)$ time and memory, requires no matrix inversion or random perturbations, and filters shot noise through temporal Welford accumulation. 

For hardware-efficient ans\"atze parameterized by single-qubit Pauli rotation gates $U_i(\theta_i) = \exp(-i \frac{\theta_i}{2} P_i)$ with $P_i \in \{X, Y, Z\}$, the generator eigenvalues are $\pm 1/2$. As a result, the energy expectation value along coordinate $\theta_i$ is a single-frequency sinusoid:
\begin{equation}
E(\theta_i) = A_i \cos(\theta_i + \phi_i) + C_i,
\label{eq:sinusoid_form}
\end{equation}
where $A_i, \phi_i, C_i$ depend on the remaining parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\ne i}$. Differentiating analytically yields gradient $g_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -A_i \sin(\theta_i + \phi_i)$ and second derivative $H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial \theta_i^2} = -A_i \cos(\theta_i + \phi_i) = -(E(\boldsymbol{\theta}) - C_i)$. Evaluating the trigonometric identity at shifts $\pm \pi/2$ reveals that the central difference:
\begin{equation}
H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2}\left(E\left(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \frac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_i\right) + E\left(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \frac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_i\right) - 2E(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)
\label{eq:exact_hessian}
\end{equation}
is algebraically exact \cite{wierichs2022general}. Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_hessian} requires only the unshifted evaluation $E(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, because the forward and backward shifted evaluations $E(\boldsymbol{\theta} \pm \frac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_i)$ are already executed by the quantum processor to compute the parameter-shift gradient $g_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:param_shift}. The total per-step cost is therefore $2p+1$ circuits. In our benchmark, we evaluate this instantaneous second-order signal directly through two independent baselines: a diagonal Hessian-magnitude baseline (\textbf{Hess-Mag}, scaling the update by $|H_{ii}| + \lambda_{\text{reg}}$) and a Levenberg--Marquardt safeguarded diagonal Newton method (\textbf{Diag-Hessian}). We note that Hess-Mag serves strictly as an instantaneous classical curvature baseline and remains distinct from the quantum-Fisher-derived Hamiltonian-aware QNG of Shi \& Wang \cite{shi2025hamiltonian}.

A practical question arises: \emph{if $H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ can be evaluated at $2p+1$ circuits, why estimate curvature using gradient trajectory variance $\hat{\sigma}_i^2$ rather than $H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$?} Three reasons motivate this choice:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strict $2p$ Circuit Budget:} Standard parameter-shift gradients (Eq.~\eqref{eq:param_shift}) require $2p$ circuit evaluations. The extra unshifted circuit $E(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is needed only by Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_hessian} to complete the central difference (or to record cost history). SIGMA-QGD works directly on gradient vectors $\mathbf{g}_t$ and can run in strictly $2p$ circuits per step when unshifted evaluations are omitted.
    \item \textbf{Broad Applicability Across Generators:} Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_hessian} produces $H_{ii}$ from two shifts only when gate generators have eigenvalues $\pm 1/2$. For multi-qubit gates, fermionic excitation generators (such as UCCSD or $k$-UpCCGSD), or QAOA mixers with broader spectra, analytic second derivatives require 4- or 8-point shift formulas \cite{wierichs2022general}, doubling or quadrupling quantum runs. In contrast, SIGMA-QGD tracks trajectory variance without any extra circuits across any parameterized ansatz.
    \item \textbf{Stability Across Near-Zero Inflection Points:} In periodic quantum landscapes, instantaneous second derivatives $H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$ swing rapidly, change sign, and pass through zero ($H_{ii} \approx 0$). In instantaneous schemes (Diag-Hessian and Hess-Mag), the inverse factor $(|H_{ii}| + \lambda_{\text{reg}})^{-1}$ spikes at these crossings, driving updates off course unless dampened heavily. SIGMA-QGD avoids this vulnerability: $\hat{\sigma}_i^2(t)$ aggregates gradient spread over past iterations, creating a strictly positive curvature memory that never collapses at isolated inflection points. Our ablation tests (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_results}) verify this behavior: removing momentum from SIGMA-QGD ($\beta=0$) drops accuracy to $\Delta E \approx 0.4202$ (10q) and $0.5544$ (12q) on TFIM, directly matching Diag-Hessian ($0.4384$, $0.5609$) and Hess-Mag ($0.4431$, $0.5497$). This result demonstrates that temporal trajectory accumulation is vital for reliable preconditioning in quantum landscapes.
\end{itemize}

Recent studies have also tested hybrid schemes combining exact parameter-shift gradients with simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation (QN-SPSA+PSR)~\cite{qnspsa_psr_2024} to estimate natural gradient metrics cheaply. However, simultaneous stochastic perturbations introduce persistent sampling noise that hinders asymptotic convergence on multi-parameter spin chains. Related work exploring directional forward gradients and Riemannian geometry, such as QUIVER and Exact Geodesic Transport (EGT), provides alternative perspectives on landscape structure. Directional forward gradients, however, rely on random projection directions that add stochastic sampling noise, while exact geometric transport methods require analytically tractable circuits rather than general parameterized ans\"atze. SIGMA-QGD avoids these limitations: it operates deterministically on standard parameter-shift gradient vectors, extracting curvature directly from the optimization trajectory.

\subsection{Quasi-Newton Methods and Line-Search Constraints}
Classical deterministic quasi-Newton algorithms (such as L-BFGS-B and non-linear Conjugate Gradient) are standard tools in noiseless optimization. In physical VQE pipelines, however, quantum execution costs severely restrict their use. Quasi-Newton secant updates and Conjugate Gradient step calculations require line searches (such as Armijo backtracking or Wolfe conditions) to maintain positive-definiteness ($s_k^\top y_k > 0$) and ensure descent. In VQE, each line-search test requires running independent quantum state preparation and measurement circuits. An adaptive line search averaging $3\text{--}5$ trial steps per iteration increases the circuit count by $300\%\text{--}500\%$, breaking fixed hardware budget parity. Under finite sampling shot noise, energy fluctuations also cause Wolfe conditions to fail or cycle indefinitely. Because of these constraints, gradient-based updates with analytic step sizes or momentum schedules are standard practice in budget-matched VQE benchmarking under fixed evaluation constraints ($2p+1$ circuits/step).

\section{Methodology and Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Problem Setup and Notation}
Let $H = \sum_k c_k P_k$ be a molecular or spin Hamiltonian represented as a linear combination of Pauli strings $P_k \in \{I, X, Y, Z\}^{\otimes n}$. We consider a hardware-efficient ansatz \cite{kandala2017hardware} with $n$ qubits and $r$ repeated layers. Each layer consists of parameterized single-qubit $RY(\theta_{l,j})$ rotations followed by a linear entangling chain of CNOT gates, yielding $p = n \cdot r$ trainable parameters.

Parameters are initialized i.i.d.\ from $\text{Uniform}(-\pi/8, \pi/8)$. The partial derivative with respect to parameter $\theta_i$ is evaluated via the exact parameter-shift rule:
\begin{equation}
g_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ E\left(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \frac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_i\right) - E\left(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \frac{\pi}{2}\mathbf{e}_i\right) \right].
\label{eq:param_shift}
\end{equation}
Throughout this paper, $\mathbf{a} \oslash \mathbf{b}$ denotes Hadamard (elementwise) division: $(\mathbf{a} \oslash \mathbf{b})_i = a_i / b_i$.

\subsection{Online Trajectory Variance Estimation}
SIGMA-QGD maintains an online Bessel-corrected Welford accumulator \cite{welford1962note} for each parameter coordinate $i \in \{1, \dots, p\}$. At step $t \ge 1$, given gradient vector $\mathbf{g}_t$:
\begin{align}
M_{t,i} &= M_{t-1,i} + \frac{g_{t,i} - M_{t-1,i}}{t}, \label{eq:welford_m} \\
S_{t,i} &= S_{t-1,i} + (g_{t,i} - M_{t-1,i})(g_{t,i} - M_{t,i}). \label{eq:welford_s}
\end{align}
For $t \ge 2$, the unbiased sample variance is given by:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\sigma}_i^2(t) = \frac{S_{t,i}}{t - 1}, \quad \text{with } \hat{\sigma}_i^2(1) \equiv 0.
\label{eq:welford_var}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Preconditioner Formulation}
We define the coordinate curvature proxy $F_i$ and the preconditioned update vector $\mathbf{d}_t$ as:
\begin{equation}
F_i(t) = c \cdot \hat{\sigma}_i^2(t) + \lambda_{\text{reg}}, \qquad \mathbf{d}_t = \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t \oslash \mathbf{F}(t),
\label{eq:preconditioned_update}
\end{equation}
where $c > 0$ is the preconditioner multiplier ($c = 1.0$), $\lambda_{\text{reg}} > 0$ is the default regularization constant ($\lambda_{\text{reg}} = 0.005$, selected via sensitivity analysis to provide a robust curvature floor), and $\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t$ is the bias-corrected momentum:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m}_t = \beta \mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1-\beta)\mathbf{g}_t, \quad \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{1 - \beta^t},
\label{eq:momentum}
\end{equation}
with parameter-adaptive momentum decay:
\begin{equation}
\beta = \operatorname{clip}\Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\max(p, 4)}}, \, 0.5, \, 0.98\Bigr).
\label{eq:beta_formula}
\end{equation}
As detailed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:sigma_qgd}, prior to updating Eqs.~\eqref{eq:welford_m}--\eqref{eq:welford_s} and~\eqref{eq:momentum}, $\mathbf{g}_t$ is safeguarded via global $\ell_2$-norm clipping $\mathbf{g}_{c,t} = \mathbf{g}_t \cdot \min(1, \sqrt{p}/\|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2)$, which coincides with $\mathbf{g}_t$ whenever $\|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2 \le \sqrt{p}$.

\subsection{Theoretical Guarantees}
We now formalize the conditions under which trajectory variance accurately proxies local curvature and guarantees descent.

Let $E: \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}$ be twice continuously differentiable with gradient $\mathbf{g} = \nabla E$ and Hessian $H = \nabla^2 E$. Consider a sliding window $W_t \subset \{1, \dots, t\}$ of $w$ steps ending at iteration $t$, with temporal variance:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\sigma}_i^2(t) = \frac{1}{w-1} \sum_{\tau \in W_t} \left( g_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau) - \bar{g}_i(W_t) \right)^2, \quad \bar{g}_i(W_t) = \frac{1}{w} \sum_{\tau \in W_t} g_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau).
\end{equation}

\begin{assumption}[Local Quadraticity]
\label{ass:quadraticity}
On window $W_t$, $E$ satisfies a second-order Taylor expansion about reference point $\boldsymbol{\theta}^* \in W_t$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau) = \mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) + H(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*) + \mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau), \quad \|\mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau)\| \le \frac{L}{2} \|\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $L < \infty$ is the local Lipschitz constant of the Hessian.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Aggregate Diagonal Dominance]
\label{ass:diag_dominance}
Off-diagonal Hessian elements are bounded by $|H_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)| \le \delta$ for all $i \ne j$, satisfying the aggregate dominance condition $\delta \sqrt{p} \ll |H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)|$ for the active coordinates.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Bounded Gradient and Curvature]
\label{ass:bounded_curvature}
The coordinate gradient and diagonal Hessian elements are bounded: $|g_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})| \le M$ and $|H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta})| \le M$ for all $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and coordinate indices $i$. For hardware-efficient trigonometric ansatzes, $M$ is bounded by $\sum_k |c_k|$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Bounded Window Displacement]
\label{ass:displacement}
The trajectory displacement within window $W_t$ is bounded by $\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*\| \le r$ for all $\tau \in W_t$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Coordinate-wise Momentum Alignment]
\label{ass:alignment}
At step $t$, the bias-corrected momentum and negative gradient agree in sign coordinate-wise:
\begin{equation}
\hat{m}_{t,i} (-g_{t,i}) \ge 0 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, p\}.
\end{equation}
\end{assumption}

\begin{remark}[Aggregate Alignment vs. Coordinate-wise Margin]
A positive overall inner product $\langle \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t, -\mathbf{g}_t \rangle > 0$ does not guarantee that a diagonal preconditioner will descend. We can see this in a simple two-dimensional example. Take $\mathbf{F} = (0.1, 0.005)^\top$ and $\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t \odot (-\mathbf{g}_t) = (1.0, -0.5)^\top$. The unscaled vectors align:
\[
\langle \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t, -\mathbf{g}_t \rangle = 1.0 - 0.5 = 0.5 > 0.
\]
Yet the preconditioned inner product turns negative:
\[
\langle \mathbf{d}_t, -\mathbf{g}_t \rangle = \frac{1.0}{0.1} + \frac{-0.5}{0.005} = 10 - 100 = -90 < 0.
\]
The second coordinate has an opposing sign. Because its preconditioner $F_i$ is tiny, that coordinate blows up and drives the update uphill. While coordinate-wise alignment (Assumption~\ref{ass:alignment}) offers a simple sufficient test, the true necessary and sufficient criterion is the margin condition in Proposition~\ref{prop:descent}.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Local Secant Relation]
\label{lem:secant}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:quadraticity}, \ref{ass:diag_dominance}, and \ref{ass:displacement}, the gradient difference satisfies:
\begin{equation}
g_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau) - g_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) = H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)(\theta_{\tau,i} - \theta_i^*) + \varepsilon_{i,\tau},
\end{equation}
where $|\varepsilon_{i,\tau}| \le \delta r \sqrt{p} + \frac{L}{2} r^2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Decomposing the Taylor expansion coordinate-wise:
\begin{equation}
g_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau) - g_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) = H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)(\theta_{\tau,i} - \theta_i^*) + \sum_{j \ne i} H_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)(\theta_{\tau,j} - \theta_j^*) + R_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau).
\end{equation}
By Cauchy--Schwarz and Assumption~\ref{ass:diag_dominance}:
\begin{equation}
\left| \sum_{j \ne i} H_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)(\theta_{\tau,j} - \theta_j^*) \right| \le \delta \sum_{j=1}^p |\theta_{\tau,j} - \theta_j^*| \le \delta \sqrt{p} \|\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*\| \le \delta r \sqrt{p}.
\end{equation}
Combining this with $\|R_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau)\| \le \|\mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau)\| \le \frac{L}{2} r^2$ yields the stated bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Variance--Curvature Proportionality]
\label{prop:variance_curvature}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:quadraticity}--\ref{ass:displacement}, the sample variance of the $i$-th gradient component on window $W_t$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\sigma}_i^2(t) = H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)^2 \operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i] + \mathcal{R}_i,
\end{equation}
where the residual $\mathcal{R}_i$ is rigorously bounded by:
\begin{equation}
|\mathcal{R}_i| \le 2 M r \left( \delta r \sqrt{p} + \frac{L}{2} r^2 \right) + \left( \delta r \sqrt{p} + \frac{L}{2} r^2 \right)^2 = \mathcal{O}\left( (M \delta \sqrt{p} + \delta^2 p) r^2 + M L r^3 + L^2 r^4 \right).
\label{eq:prop1_remainder}
\end{equation}
As a result, when the parameter moves actively across the window ($\operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i] = \Theta(r^2)$), the diagonal curvature term $H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)^2 \operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i]$ dominates the residual provided $\delta \sqrt{p} \ll |H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)|$ (Assumption~\ref{ass:diag_dominance}) and displacement $r$ is sufficiently small.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Applying the variance operator to the secant relation from Lemma~\ref{lem:secant}:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[g_i] = \operatorname{Var}_{W_t}\left[ H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) \theta_i + \varepsilon_i \right] = H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)^2 \operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i] + 2 H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*) \operatorname{Cov}_{W_t}(\theta_i, \varepsilon_i) + \operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\varepsilon_i].
\end{equation}
By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, $|\operatorname{Cov}_{W_t}(\theta_i, \varepsilon_i)| \le \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i]} \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\varepsilon_i]}$. Because the displacement satisfies $\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*\| \le r$, we have $|\theta_{\tau,i} - \theta_i^*| \le r$, which implies $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i]} \le r$ (up to the finite-window Bessel factor $\sqrt{\frac{w}{w-1}} = 1 + \mathcal{O}(w^{-1})$). From Lemma~\ref{lem:secant}, $\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i\|_\infty \le \delta r \sqrt{p} + \frac{L}{2} r^2$, yielding $\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\varepsilon_i]} \le \|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i\|_\infty$ (up to $1 + \mathcal{O}(w^{-1})$). Using $|H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)| \le M$, the cross-covariance term is bounded by:
\begin{equation}
2 |H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)| |\operatorname{Cov}_{W_t}(\theta_i, \varepsilon_i)| \le 2 M r \left( \delta r \sqrt{p} + \frac{L}{2} r^2 \right).
\end{equation}
Adding $\operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\varepsilon_i] \le \|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i\|_\infty^2 = (\delta r \sqrt{p} + \frac{L}{2} r^2)^2$ completes the proof of Eq.~\eqref{eq:prop1_remainder}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Dynamical Variance Scaling, Energy Rescaling, and Window Formulation]
\label{rem:variance_scaling}
Three practical points clarify how this mechanism behaves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Curvature vs. Trajectory Dampening and Hamiltonian Energy Scale:} Standard Newton-Raphson methods scale updates inversely with the first power of curvature ($H_{ii}^{-1}$). In SIGMA-QGD, the variance proxy instead links to $H_{ii}^2 \operatorname{Var}[\theta_i]$. Updates therefore scale approximately as $\hat{m}_{t,i} / (c H_{ii}^2 \operatorname{Var}[\theta_i] + \lambda_{\text{reg}})$. Along coordinates with steep curvature, rapid oscillations inflate the denominator quadratically. This curbs runaway steps and stabilizes descent. Along flat coordinates ($H_{ii} \approx 0$), the step size stays safely bounded by $\lambda_{\text{reg}}^{-1}$. In the variance-dominated regime where $d_i \sim \hat{m}_i / \hat{\sigma}_i^2 \propto 1/g_i$, the update vector is not invariant under an arbitrary scalar rescaling of the Hamiltonian ($H \to \alpha H$). This behavior matches unnormalized second-order methods. It ties good values of $c$ and $\lambda_{\text{reg}}$ to the characteristic energy scale $\sum_k |c_k|$ of the Hamiltonian. When working with arbitrary spectral norm $\Lambda = \sum_k |c_k|$, one can restore scale invariance by dividing the energy by $\Lambda$ before optimizing ($\tilde{E}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = E(\boldsymbol{\theta}) / \Lambda$) or by setting $\lambda_{\text{reg}} \propto \Lambda^2$.
    \item \textbf{Trajectory-Dependent Displacement:} The term $H_{ii}^2 \operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i]$ depends on the coordinate variance $\operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i]$ traversed by the optimizer. Because of this, $\hat{\sigma}_i^2$ reflects curvature along the actual descent path rather than at a single isolated point.
    \item \textbf{Sliding Window vs. Online Accumulating Welford Estimator:} Our proofs in Assumptions~\ref{ass:quadraticity}--\ref{ass:displacement} and Proposition~\ref{prop:variance_curvature} use a compact sliding window $W_t$ of width $w$. This models the active descent phase ($t \in [1, t_0]$) where parameter displacement is large ($r = \Theta(1)$). In practice, Algorithm~\ref{alg:sigma_qgd} runs an online Bessel-corrected Welford accumulator over all prior steps ($t \ge 2$). It requires only $\mathcal{O}(1)$ time and memory per step. When the optimizer enters a basin near a local minimum ($t > t_0$), parameter movements shrink ($\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_\tau - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*\| \to 0$). In a narrow sliding window of width $w$, displacement variance $\operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i]$ would vanish, pushing $\hat{\sigma}_i^2 \to 0$ and dropping the preconditioner to the bare floor $\lambda_{\text{reg}}$. In contrast, the full-history Welford accumulator $\mathbf{S}_t / (t-1)$ keeps memory of the curvature directions explored earlier in the trajectory. This memory prevents the preconditioner from collapsing late in the run, preserving directional scaling even as parameters slow down.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Uniform Local and Global Boundedness of the Preconditioner]
\label{prop:boundedness}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:bounded_curvature} and \ref{ass:displacement}, the diagonal preconditioner $F_i(t) = c \hat{\sigma}_i^2(t) + \lambda_{\text{reg}}$ on window $W_t$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{reg}} \le F_i(t) \le c M^2 r^2 + \lambda_{\text{reg}} + \mathcal{O}(r^2).
\end{equation}
Furthermore, for the full-history Welford accumulator in Algorithm~\ref{alg:sigma_qgd}, gradient clipping ($\|\mathbf{g}_{c,t}\|_2 \le c_{\text{norm}} = \sqrt{p}$) and Assumption~\ref{ass:bounded_curvature} ensure $|g_{c,t,i}| \le G_{\max} \equiv \min(\sqrt{p}, M)$ for all $t \ge 1$, yielding the unconditional global bound $\lambda_{\text{reg}} \le F_i(t) \le F_{\max} \equiv 2c G_{\max}^2 + \lambda_{\text{reg}}$ for all $t \ge 1$, and coordinate-wise step bounds:
\begin{equation}
\frac{|\hat{m}_{t,i}|}{F_{\max}} \le |d_{t,i}| \le \frac{|\hat{m}_{t,i}|}{\lambda_{\text{reg}}}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $\hat{\sigma}_i^2 \ge 0$, the lower bound follows from $\lambda_{\text{reg}} > 0$. The local window upper bound follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:variance_curvature} and $\operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i] \le \frac{w}{w-1} r^2$ (absorbing the Bessel factor $1 + \mathcal{O}(w^{-1})$). Globally, since $|g_{c,t,i}| \le G_{\max}$, the unbiased sample variance over any horizon $t \ge 2$ satisfies $\hat{\sigma}_i^2(t) = \frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{\tau=1}^t (g_{c,\tau,i} - M_{t,i})^2 \le \frac{t}{t-1} G_{\max}^2 \le 2 G_{\max}^2$, establishing the unconditional upper bound $F_{\max}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Descent Direction Guarantee under Margin Condition]
\label{prop:descent}
Let $\mathcal{S}_+ = \{i : \hat{m}_{t,i}(-g_{t,i}) \ge 0\}$ and $\mathcal{S}_- = \{j : \hat{m}_{t,j}(-g_{t,j}) < 0\}$ partition the coordinate indices $\{1, \dots, p\}$ at step $t$. Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:bounded_curvature} and \ref{ass:displacement}, the preconditioned update direction $\mathbf{d}_t = \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t \oslash \mathbf{F}(t)$ constitutes a strict descent direction ($\langle \mathbf{d}_t, -\mathbf{g}_t \rangle > 0$) if the aligned-coordinate projection dominates the misaligned contribution according to the margin condition:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}_+} \frac{\hat{m}_{t,i} (-g_{t,i})}{F_i(t)} > \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_-} \frac{|\hat{m}_{t,j} g_{t,j}|}{F_j(t)}.
\label{eq:margin_condition}
\end{equation}
In particular, using the uniform bounds $\lambda_{\text{reg}} \le F_k(t) \le F_{\max}$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:boundedness}, a sufficient condition is:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}_+} \hat{m}_{t,i} (-g_{t,i}) > \kappa \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_-} |\hat{m}_{t,j} g_{t,j}|, \qquad \kappa = \frac{F_{\max}}{\lambda_{\text{reg}}}.
\label{eq:kappa_margin}
\end{equation}
In the special case of coordinate-wise alignment ($\mathcal{S}_- = \emptyset$, Assumption~\ref{ass:alignment}), descent holds unconditionally with $\langle \mathbf{d}_t, -\mathbf{g}_t \rangle \ge \frac{1}{F_{\max}} \langle \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t, -\mathbf{g}_t \rangle > 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Decomposing the preconditioned inner product over the partition $\mathcal{S}_+ \cup \mathcal{S}_-$:
\begin{align}
\langle \mathbf{d}_t, -\mathbf{g}_t \rangle &= \sum_{i=1}^p \frac{\hat{m}_{t,i}(-g_{t,i})}{F_i(t)} \nonumber \\
&= \sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}_+} \frac{\hat{m}_{t,i}(-g_{t,i})}{F_i(t)} - \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_-} \frac{|\hat{m}_{t,j} g_{t,j}|}{F_j(t)}.
\end{align}
Positivity is guaranteed whenever the first sum exceeds the second, establishing Eq.~\eqref{eq:margin_condition}. Applying the uniform lower bound $\lambda_{\text{reg}}$ and upper bound $F_{\max}$ yields Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa_margin}. When $\mathcal{S}_- = \emptyset$, the negative sum vanishes and the uniform lower bound preserves strict descent.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Empirical Validity and Dynamical Self-Damping.}
While ideal coordinate-wise alignment ($\mathcal{S}_- = \emptyset$) holds in $67.10\%$ (TFIM) and $71.93\%$ (XXZ) of coordinate-step pairs, the generalized margin condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:margin_condition} reveals an intrinsic self-stabilizing mechanism of trajectory-variance preconditioning. Transient sign misalignments ($\hat{m}_{t,j} g_{t,j} > 0$) occur almost exclusively along coordinates undergoing rapid directional oscillations across steep ravine walls. Along these jittery coordinates, the temporal variance $\hat{\sigma}_j^2$ is large, automatically inflating the preconditioner denominator $F_j(t) \gg \lambda_{\text{reg}}$. Because of this, the misaligned negative terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:margin_condition} are dynamically suppressed by large preconditioner values ($1/F_j(t) \to 0$). Conversely, coordinates progressing smoothly along the valley floor exhibit stable gradients, keeping $F_i(t)$ near $\lambda_{\text{reg}}$ and amplifying positive descent progress. Across all tested models, the coordinate condition ratio $\max_{i,t} F_i(t) / \lambda_{\text{reg}}$ remains empirically bounded in $[12.4, 48.6]$, well below the theoretical worst-case ceiling $\kappa = F_{\max}/\lambda_{\text{reg}} \le 200\text{--}400$, as coordinates rarely hit theoretical limits simultaneously. Because of this dynamical self-damping, the preconditioned inner product $\langle \mathbf{d}_t, -\mathbf{g}_t \rangle$ remained strictly positive across \textbf{100.0\%} of evaluated steps ($200/200$) in all tested configurations.

\begin{theorem}[Non-Convex Stationarity Convergence Rate]
\label{thm:convergence}
Let $E: \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}$ be $L_g$-smooth with lower-bounded global minimum $E^* > -\infty$. Suppose that along the optimization trajectory, the margin condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:descent} holds with uniform alignment factor $\gamma_0 > 0$ such that $\langle \mathbf{d}_t, -\mathbf{g}_t \rangle \ge \frac{\gamma_0}{F_{\max}} \|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2^2$ (which holds unconditionally when $\beta = 0$ with $\gamma_0 = \min(1, 1/M)$, or empirically under Proposition~\ref{prop:descent} for $\beta > 0$), where $F_{\max}$ is the uniform preconditioner upper bound from Proposition~\ref{prop:boundedness}, and the base learning rate satisfies $\eta_0 < \frac{2 \gamma_0 \lambda_{\text{reg}}^2 (1-\beta)}{L_g F_{\max}}$. Then the sequence of iterates $\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}\}_{t=1}^T$ generated by Algorithm~\ref{alg:sigma_qgd} satisfies the ergodic stationary-point convergence bound:
\begin{equation}
\min_{1 \le t \le T} \|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})\|_2^2 \le \frac{E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) - E^*}{\mathcal{C} \cdot T} = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right),
\label{eq:ergodic_rate}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C} = \eta_0 \left( \frac{\gamma_0}{F_{\max}} - \frac{L_g \eta_0}{2 \lambda_{\text{reg}}^2 (1-\beta)} \right) > 0$. Furthermore, in the presence of unbiased stochastic quantum measurement shot noise with bounded variance $\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_t - \nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})\|_2^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] \le \sigma_{\text{shot}}^2$, setting a diminishing step-size schedule $\eta_t = \eta_0 / \sqrt{t}$ yields the standard non-convex stochastic stationarity rate:
\begin{equation}
\min_{1 \le t \le T} \mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})\|_2^2\right] = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right).
\label{eq:stochastic_rate}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By $L_g$-smoothness of $E(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, for consecutive parameter iterates $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t = \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} - \eta_0 \mathbf{d}_t$ with $\mathbf{g}_t = \nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})$:
\begin{equation}
E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t) \le E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}) - \eta_0 \langle \mathbf{g}_t, \mathbf{d}_t \rangle + \frac{L_g \eta_0^2}{2} \|\mathbf{d}_t\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
Applying the margin lower bound $\langle \mathbf{d}_t, -\mathbf{g}_t \rangle \ge \frac{\gamma_0}{F_{\max}} \|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2^2$ and the preconditioner lower bound $F_i(t) \ge \lambda_{\text{reg}}$ (for $t \ge t_{\text{warm}}$, and $F_i(t) = 1 \ge \lambda_{\text{reg}}$ during warmup) gives $\|\mathbf{d}_t\|_2 \le \|\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t\|_2 / \lambda_{\text{reg}}$. Summing over $t = 1, \dots, T$ yields:
\begin{equation}
E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_T) - E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) \le -\frac{\eta_0 \gamma_0}{F_{\max}} \sum_{t=1}^T \|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2^2 + \frac{L_g \eta_0^2}{2 \lambda_{\text{reg}}^2} \sum_{t=1}^T \|\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
Since $\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t = \sum_{\tau=1}^t w_{t,\tau} \mathbf{g}_{c,\tau}$ with convex weights $w_{t,\tau} = \frac{(1-\beta)\beta^{t-\tau}}{1-\beta^t}$ summing to $1$, $\|\mathbf{g}_{c,\tau}\|_2 \le \|\mathbf{g}_\tau\|_2$, and $1 - \beta^t \ge 1 - \beta$, Jensen's inequality gives $\|\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t\|_2^2 \le \sum_{\tau=1}^t w_{t,\tau} \|\mathbf{g}_\tau\|_2^2 \le \sum_{\tau=1}^t \beta^{t-\tau} \|\mathbf{g}_\tau\|_2^2$. Summing over $t=1,\dots,T$ and exchanging summation order yields $\sum_{t=1}^T \|\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t\|_2^2 \le \frac{1}{1-\beta} \sum_{t=1}^T \|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2^2$. Substituting this bound and rearranging with $E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_T) \ge E^*$ gives:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C} \sum_{t=1}^T \|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2^2 \le E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) - E^*,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C} > 0$ under the stated step-size condition. Bounding $\sum_{t=1}^T \|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2^2 \ge T \min_{1 \le t \le T} \|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2^2$ and dividing by $\mathcal{C} T$ establishes Eq.~\eqref{eq:ergodic_rate}. Under additive zero-mean shot noise with bounded variance $\sigma_{\text{shot}}^2$, note that the Welford update satisfies $|\hat{\sigma}_i^2(t) - \hat{\sigma}_i^2(t-1)| = \mathcal{O}(G_{\max}^2 / t)$, so replacing $F_i(t)$ with the $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$-measurable lagged preconditioner $F_i(t-1)$ incurs at most an $\mathcal{O}(\eta_t / t)$ bias per step (whose sum $\sum_{t=1}^T \eta_t / t < \infty$ is bounded), while conditional expectations introduce an $\mathcal{O}(L_g \eta_t^2 \sigma_{\text{shot}}^2)$ variance penalty per step; applying $\eta_t = \eta_0 / \sqrt{t}$ yields $\min_{1 \le t \le T} \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})\|_2^2] = \mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})$~\cite{ghadimi2013stochastic}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Warmup Schedule and Complete Algorithm}
To prevent early noisy variance estimates from destabilizing the updates, the algorithm turns on preconditioning only after a warmup window:
\begin{equation}
t_{\text{warm}} = \min(3p, \max(10, \lfloor T / 5 \rfloor)).
\label{eq:warmup}
\end{equation}
For $t < t_{\text{warm}}$, the update follows standard momentum-accelerated gradient descent ($\mathbf{d}_t = \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t$). Evaluating the unshifted energy $E_t = E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})$ adds 1 circuit per step for tracking the best iterate, bringing the logged experimental budget to $2p+1$. When only parameter updates are needed, $E_t$ can be omitted to execute in strictly $2p$ circuits per step.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{SIGMA-QGD Optimization Algorithm}
\label{alg:sigma_qgd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Initial parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^p$, learning rate $\eta_0$, multiplier $c=1.0$, regularizer $\lambda_{\text{reg}}=0.005$, max steps $T$.
\STATE Initialize accumulators $\mathbf{M} \leftarrow \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{S} \leftarrow \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{m} \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$, best energy $E^* \leftarrow \infty, \boldsymbol{\theta}^* \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta}_0$.
\STATE Compute $t_{\text{warm}} = \min(3p, \max(10, \lfloor T/5 \rfloor))$, clip threshold $c_{\text{norm}} = \sqrt{p}$.
\FOR{$t = 1$ \TO $T$}
    \STATE Evaluate cost and parameter-shift gradient: $E_t, \mathbf{g}_t \leftarrow \text{CostAndGradient}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})$ \hfill \COMMENT{$2p+1$ circuits}
    \STATE Gradient clipping: $\mathbf{g}_c \leftarrow \mathbf{g}_t \cdot \min(1, c_{\text{norm}} / \|\mathbf{g}_t\|_2)$
    \STATE Update Welford accumulators $(\mathbf{M}, \mathbf{S})$ with $\mathbf{g}_c$ via Eqs.~\eqref{eq:welford_m}--\eqref{eq:welford_s}
    \STATE Compute unbiased variance $\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^2 \leftarrow \mathbf{S} / (t-1)$ for $t \ge 2$, else $\mathbf{0}$
    \STATE Update momentum: $\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \beta \mathbf{m} + (1-\beta)\mathbf{g}_c$, $\hat{\mathbf{m}} \leftarrow \mathbf{m} / (1-\beta^t)$
    \IF{$t \ge t_{\text{warm}}$}
        \STATE $\mathbf{F} \leftarrow c \hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^2 + \lambda_{\text{reg}} \mathbf{1}$
        \STATE $\mathbf{d}_t \leftarrow \hat{\mathbf{m}} \oslash \mathbf{F}$
    \ELSE
        \STATE $\mathbf{d}_t \leftarrow \hat{\mathbf{m}}$
    \ENDIF
    \STATE $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} - \eta_0 \mathbf{d}_t$
    \IF{$E_t < E^*$}
        \STATE $E^* \leftarrow E_t, \boldsymbol{\theta}^* \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}$
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\RETURN Optimal parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$, minimum energy $E^*$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Benchmark Hamiltonians}
We test the algorithms on two standard spin chains with periodic boundary conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1D Transverse-Field Ising Model (TFIM):}
    \begin{equation}
    H_{\text{TFIM}} = -J \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i Z_{i+1} - h \sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \quad J = 1.0, \, h = 0.5.
    \end{equation}
    Exact ground states: $E_0 = -10.6356$ ($n=10$), $E_0 = -12.7626$ ($n=12$).
    \item \textbf{1D Heisenberg XXZ Spin Chain:}
    \begin{equation}
    H_{\text{XXZ}} = J_{xy} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i X_{i+1} + Y_i Y_{i+1}) + J_z \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i Z_{i+1} + h \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i,
    \end{equation}
    with $J_{xy} = 1.0, J_z = 0.5, h = 0.1$. Exact ground states: $E_0 = -15.2761$ ($n=10$), $E_0 = -18.2291$ ($n=12$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ansatz and Initialization}
We set up a hardware-efficient ansatz of depth $r=3$. This layout gives $p=30$ trainable rotation angles for $n=10$ qubits and $p=36$ angles for $n=12$ qubits. In every trial, we draw the initial parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$ from a uniform distribution ($\theta_{0,i} \sim \text{Uniform}(-\pi/8, \pi/8)$). All competing optimizers start from identical random seeds to keep comparisons direct and paired.

\subsection{Baseline Algorithms and Circuit Accounting}
We test 11 optimizers in total (SIGMA-QGD alongside 10 baselines) while tracking every quantum circuit call:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-order baselines ($2p+1$ circuits/step):} Adam \cite{kingma2014adam}, AdaBelief \cite{zhuang2020adabelief}, RMSProp \cite{tieleman2012lecture}, and Yogi \cite{zaheer2018adaptive}.
    \item \textbf{Exact second-order baselines ($2p+1$ circuits/step):} Hess-Mag and Diag-Hessian (via Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_hessian} \cite{wierichs2022general}).
    \item \textbf{Quantum Natural Gradient ($3p+2$ circuits/step):} Diag-QNG \cite{stokes2020quantum,wierichs2020avoiding}.
    \item \textbf{Derivative-free baselines:} COBYLA ($1$ call/step \cite{powell1994direct}), SPSA ($3$ calls/step \cite{spall1992multivariate,spall1997accelerated,spall1998implementation,spall2000adaptive}), QN-SPSA ($9$ calls/step \cite{gacon2021simultaneous}), and CMA-ES (budget-matched \cite{novak2025benchmarking}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Statistical Significance Testing}
To measure differences between SIGMA-QGD and each baseline, we run two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. We adjust all reported $p$-values with the Holm--Bonferroni step-down correction to control family-wise error across the 10 pairwise comparisons.

    \item \textbf{High-Fidelity Tracking in Commuting-Field Regimes (TFIM):} On the TFIM spin chain, trajectory variance tracks the exact diagonal Hessian closely in log-space ($r = 0.5665 \pm 0.0577$ at 10 qubits, rising to $r = 0.6256 \pm 0.0301$ at 12 qubits, both $p = 6.1 \times 10^{-5}$). This rise in correlation with system size shows that the variance proxy scales favorably, capturing local curvature as the Hilbert space quadruples ($2^{10} \to 2^{12}$).
    \item \textbf{Statistically Significant Signal under Frustrated Exchange (XXZ):} On the non-commuting XXZ chain, the Pearson correlation remains positive and statistically significant ($r = 0.3196 \pm 0.0829$ at 10 qubits, $r = 0.2938 \pm 0.0914$ at 12 qubits, $p = 6.1 \times 10^{-5}$). Spearman rank correlations are also positive ($\rho = 0.1162$, $p = 0.010$ at 10 qubits; $\rho = 0.1113$, $p = 0.004$ at 12 qubits).
    \item \textbf{Physical Origin of Model Discrepancy:} The gap in correlation between TFIM ($r \approx 0.60$) and XXZ ($r \approx 0.31$) reflects the Hamiltonian operator structure. In TFIM, nearest-neighbor $Z_i Z_{i+1}$ terms commute with each other, and the transverse field $X_i$ dominates along coordinate axes. This structure keeps off-diagonal Hessian couplings small ($\delta \sqrt{p} \ll |H_{ii}|$, matching Assumption~\ref{ass:diag_dominance}). In XXZ, the non-commuting exchange terms $X_i X_{i+1} + Y_i Y_{i+1}$ introduce strong off-diagonal coupling that limits how much curvature is visible through diagonal projections alone. This finding matches analytical results on Hessian scaling in variational quantum algorithms~\cite{hessian_resolvability_2026}, which show that non-commuting terms suppress resolvable diagonal modes and inflate off-diagonal couplings across the parameter manifold.
    \item \textbf{Scale Invariance of Log-Space Correlation:} The preconditioned update in Eq.~\eqref{eq:preconditioned_update} uses $\hat{\sigma}_i^2$ directly, and Proposition~\ref{prop:variance_curvature} links $\hat{\sigma}_i^2 \propto H_{ii}^2$. Still, we evaluate the proxy in log-space ($\log \hat{\sigma}_i^2$ vs. $\log |H_{ii}|$). In periodic quantum circuits, coordinate curvatures and gradient variances span several orders of magnitude (from $10^{-4}$ near plateaus to $10^0$ near steep valleys). Linear correlations on such heavy-tailed data are distorted by rare outliers. Evaluating in log-space gives a robust, scale-invariant check of whether the proxy preserves the correct relative curvature hierarchy across parameters.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Performance on the Commuting TFIM Chain}
On the Transverse-Field Ising model, SIGMA-QGD gains a clear accuracy advantage over all 10 baselines. At $n=10$ qubits, SIGMA-QGD reaches a mean energy gap of $\Delta E = 0.0092 \pm 0.0019$. Closest adaptive competitors---AdaBelief ($0.0907 \pm 0.1168$), Adam ($0.1074 \pm 0.1128$), and Yogi ($0.1111 \pm 0.1087$)---record gaps $9.9\times$, $11.7\times$, and $12.1\times$ larger. At $n=12$ qubits ($p=36$), SIGMA-QGD scales steadily to $\Delta E = 0.0112 \pm 0.0013$, whereas AdaBelief ($0.1421$), Adam ($0.1700$), and Yogi ($0.1861$) degrade to $12.7\times$, $15.2\times$, and $16.6\times$ higher errors.

Wilcoxon signed-rank tests in Table~\ref{tab:main_benchmark_consolidated} confirm that SIGMA-QGD is statistically superior to every baseline on TFIM at 10 and 12 qubits ($p_{\text{Holm}} < 10^{-4}$ across all 10 comparisons). The standard deviation of SIGMA-QGD ($\sigma = 0.0019$ at 10q, $\sigma = 0.0013$ at 12q) is $50\times\text{--}90\times$ smaller than Adam ($\sigma = 0.1128$) and AdaBelief ($\sigma = 0.1168$), lowering sample variance by over three orders of magnitude. The final energy distributions in Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark_boxplots}(a)--(b) explain this gap: for Adam, AdaBelief, and Yogi, the sample mean is pushed up by $15\%\text{--}20\%$ of runs getting trapped in local minima ($\Delta E \approx 0.3\text{--}0.5$), even though their medians are lower ($\approx 0.05\text{--}0.08$). By comparison, SIGMA-QGD exhibits zero trapping across all $20/20$ independent seeds: its median ($\Delta E_{\text{med}} = 0.0090$ at 10q, $0.0110$ at 12q) coincides with its sample mean to three decimal places, with an interquartile range (IQR) below $0.002$. This tight clustering reflects the physical efficacy of the trajectory variance proxy: because $Z_i Z_{i+1}$ terms commute and off-diagonal cross-talk is weak, the online variance proxy correlates strongly with the exact diagonal curvature ($r = 0.567$, accounting for $r^2 \approx 32.1\%$ of log-diagonal curvature variance at 10 qubits, and $r = 0.626$, $r^2 \approx 39.1\%$ at 12 qubits, as established in Section~\ref{sec:exp_proxy}), preventing the premature stalling and directional thrashing that plague unconditioned optimizers.

\subsubsection{Performance on the Frustrated Heisenberg XXZ Chain}
The Heisenberg XXZ model provides a tough optimization testbed. Its non-commuting terms ($X_i X_{i+1} + Y_i Y_{i+1}$) generate strong qubit entanglement and dense off-diagonal curvature. Even with this frustration, SIGMA-QGD reaches the lowest mean energy gap across all 11 methods in both system sizes: $\Delta E = 1.4889 \pm 0.7845$ at 10 qubits and $\Delta E = 1.8408 \pm 0.8336$ at 12 qubits.

At 10 qubits, SIGMA-QGD significantly outperforms Adam ($2.0897$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.3 \times 10^{-3}$), AdaBelief ($2.0853$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.006$), RMSProp ($2.0988$, $p_{\text{Holm}} < 10^{-4}$), Yogi ($2.1026$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.002$), Hess-Mag ($2.1303$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.006$), and Diag-Hessian ($2.1888$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.006$). SIGMA-QGD also outperforms Diag-QNG ($1.6895$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.004$), even though Diag-QNG consumes $50\%$ more quantum circuit evaluations ($18,400$ vs. $12,200$). 

At 12 qubits, SIGMA-QGD maintains the lowest mean energy gap among all 11 evaluated algorithms ($\Delta E = 1.8408 \pm 0.8336$). Under Holm--Bonferroni corrections, its advantage is statistically significant over derivative-free and stochastic techniques (COBYLA, SPSA, QN-SPSA, all $p_{\text{Holm}} < 10^{-4}$) and over the instantaneous Hessian baseline Hess-Mag ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.045$). When compared with Adam ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.07$), Diag-Hessian ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.10$), AdaBelief ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.16$), Yogi ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.16$), and RMSProp ($p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.52$), SIGMA-QGD delivers lower mean error with overlapping error bars on this frustrated surface. It also matches Diag-QNG ($1.9602$, $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.49$) in energy while using $34\%$ fewer circuits ($14,600$ vs. $22,000$), demonstrating superior practical efficiency.

\paragraph{Budget-Matched Parity and Line-Search Constraints in Quantum Optimization.}
One might ask why second-order baselines like Diag-Hessian and Hess-Mag omit adaptive line searches (such as Armijo backtracking) or trust-region steps. The reason is circuit cost. On quantum hardware, checking the objective at trial points along an update ray $\boldsymbol{\theta} + \alpha \mathbf{d}$ requires fresh state preparation and measurement runs. Testing 3--5 trial points per iteration would increase total circuit usage by $300\%\text{--}500\%$. That would break our $2p+1$ per-step budget constraint. Furthermore, measurement shot noise makes Armijo tests unreliable; random energy fluctuations cause the condition to accept bad steps or loop endlessly. To keep comparisons fair and equal in budget, we tested each gradient method using its tuned optimal learning rate $\eta^*$ (Section~\ref{sec:lr_sweep_results}) alongside standard Levenberg-Marquardt damping ($\lambda_{\text{reg}} = 0.005$). Diag-Hessian struggles because trigonometric quantum landscapes oscillate rapidly. When instantaneous second derivatives $H_{ii}$ approach zero or go negative, the inverse multiplier surges ($g_i / \lambda_{\text{reg}}$), throwing the parameters out of the basin unless momentum and clipping rein it in.

\subsubsection{Comparison with Stochastic and Natural Gradient Baselines}
Simultaneous perturbation methods such as SPSA and QN-SPSA use few circuits ($3$ and $9$ calls per step, respectively). In multi-parameter manifolds ($p=30\text{--}36$), however, their stochastic metric approximations suffer from heavy Monte Carlo variance, leaving large optimality gaps. Deterministic methods like Diag-QNG~\cite{stokes2020quantum} avoid stochastic collapse, but require $3p+2$ circuits per step ($18,400\text{--}22,000$ circuits in total). SIGMA-QGD avoids this trade-off: it extracts local diagonal curvature from the \emph{temporal variance} of standard parameter-shift evaluations, reaching high ground-state precision within the standard $2p+1$ circuit budget.

To test Diag-QNG under an identical circuit limit, we also examined its performance when stopped at the exact circuit budget given to SIGMA-QGD ($12,200$ circuits at 10q and $14,600$ circuits at 12q). That budget caps Diag-QNG at $T_{\text{QNG}} = \lfloor 12,200/92 \rfloor = 132$ iterations for 10 qubits and $\lfloor 14,600/110 \rfloor = 132$ iterations for 12 qubits. With equal circuit counts, Diag-QNG yields mean energy gaps of $\Delta E \approx 0.428$ (TFIM 10q), $0.441$ (TFIM 12q), $1.821$ (XXZ 10q), and $2.115$ (XXZ 12q). In Table~\ref{tab:main_benchmark_consolidated}, Diag-QNG had a $50\%$ circuit advantage ($T=200$) and still converged to higher energies. Enforcing strict circuit parity makes SIGMA-QGD's lead wider across all four benchmarks.

\subsubsection{Circuit Pareto Frontier Analysis}
Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark_efficiency} shows the empirical Pareto frontier, plotting total quantum circuits against final log energy gap ($\log_{10} \Delta E$). Optimizers toward the lower-left corner deliver the best balance between execution time and solution accuracy.

Zero-order schemes (COBYLA at $200$ circuits, SPSA at $600$ circuits) finish rapidly but stall far from the ground state ($\Delta E > 0.5$ on TFIM, $\Delta E > 3.5$ on XXZ). At the other extreme, Diag-QNG consumes $50\%$ more circuits ($18,400$ circuits at 10q, $22,000$ at 12q) because of its extra metric evaluations. Even with that surplus, its TFIM gap ($0.4068$) is $44\times$ worse than SIGMA-QGD ($0.0092$) because it lacks momentum damping. SIGMA-QGD anchors the high-precision Pareto front in all four test cases. It finds the lowest energy while staying strictly within the standard gradient budget ($12,200$ circuits at 10q, $14,600$ circuits at 12q). Only COBYLA shares the frontier, occupying the low-cost 200-circuit corner.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Temporal Smoothing vs. Curvature Proxy Identity}
Does SIGMA-QGD outperform same-budget second-order baselines because of \emph{what} it measures (gradient variance rather than instantaneous curvature) or because of \emph{how} it gathers data over time (a temporal running average instead of a single-point snapshot)? Both factors matter, but their roles differ.

Hess-Mag and Diag-Hessian compute the exact diagonal second derivatives $H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)$ at each step using Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_hessian}. On non-convex quantum surfaces, especially with non-commuting Pauli operators or shot noise, these instantaneous derivatives change wildly between iterations. Near inflection points where $H_{ii}$ drops close to zero, the unconditioned inverse factor $1/(|H_{ii}| + \lambda_{\text{reg}})$ spikes violently. This surge triggers wild parameter jumps and derails optimization.

One might ask whether adding first-order momentum to instantaneous Hessian baselines (similar to quasi-Newton methods with Polyak or EMA momentum) would fix this instability. In practice, it cannot. When the instantaneous second derivative drops near zero ($H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t) \approx 0$), the diagonal scaling factor $(|H_{ii}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)| + \lambda_{\text{reg}})^{-1}$ hits its upper ceiling $\lambda_{\text{reg}}^{-1} = 200$ (for $\lambda_{\text{reg}} = 0.005$). Multiplying this large factor by a momentum vector $\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t$ does not calm the update; instead, it creates a $200\times$ step-size jump along coordinate $i$, pushing parameters out of the local basin. By comparison, SIGMA-QGD scales updates using temporal variance $\hat{\sigma}_i^2(t)$, which gathers gradient spread over the full descent history. Because the sum of squared deviations $\sum_{\tau=1}^t (g_{c,\tau,i} - \bar{g}_{t,i})^2$ grows steadily along any active trajectory, $\hat{\sigma}_i^2(t)$ never drops to zero at isolated inflection points. This completely avoids the singular spikes seen in instantaneous Hessian inversion.

SIGMA-QGD takes a different route by computing the running variance $\hat{\sigma}_i^2 = \operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[g_i]$ with Welford's algorithm along the path taken. This design provides two key benefits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-Pass Curvature Regularization:} Gathering variance over the parameter path filters out high-frequency gradient jitter without running additional quantum circuits.
    \item \textbf{Path-Integrated Sensitivity vs. Static Point Curvature:} As shown in Proposition~\ref{prop:variance_curvature}, $\hat{\sigma}_i^2 \approx H_{ii}^2 \operatorname{Var}_{W_t}[\theta_i]$. Instead of sampling point curvature at an isolated coordinate $\boldsymbol{\theta}_t$, trajectory variance captures parameter sensitivity along the entire descent path. Parameters that swung through large gradient shifts receive smaller, safer step sizes, fitting the update directly to the traversed terrain.
\end{itemize}
Temporal smoothing is not an accidental byproduct. It is a core structural feature that stabilizes optimization across quantum landscapes without extra circuit executions. This agrees with earlier theoretical studies of curvature regularization in variational algorithms \cite{stokes2020quantum,wierichs2020avoiding}, which showed that raw second-order updates easily diverge on non-convex surfaces unless damped along poorly conditioned coordinates.

\subsection{Ansatz Expressivity and Landscape Frustration}
On the Heisenberg XXZ Hamiltonian, diagonal QNG and adaptive first-order baselines reach final energy gaps close to SIGMA-QGD ($\Delta E \approx 1.5\text{--}1.8$). This remaining gap highlights the boundary between what an optimizer can find and what a circuit can express:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ansatz Expressivity vs. Optimization Limits:} The 1D Heisenberg XXZ chain has gapless critical ground states and entangled structures that a shallow $r=3$ hardware-efficient $RY$-CNOT circuit cannot capture exactly. The residual gap of $\sim 1.5$ stems from the expressivity limit of the circuit family itself, not from early convergence failure.
    \item \textbf{Shallow NISQ Regimes vs. Barren Plateaus:} Deeper circuits ($r \ge 6$) could express the state more accurately, but they run into noise-induced barren plateaus on present-day quantum processors. By testing on this shallow, frustrated model, we test whether optimizers can locate the best parameter set possible for a realistic circuit without stalling in false local minima or oscillating out of control.
    \item \textbf{Boundary of Diagonal Approximations:} Our results also mark where diagonal curvature methods reach their limit. In models dominated by non-local Pauli exchanges, diagonal matrices capture only part of the full geometric curvature. This explains why diagonal QNG and SIGMA-QGD eventually settle onto similar energy plateaus.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}
We note two main boundaries of our study:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ansatz Specificity:} Our benchmarks focused on hardware-efficient $RY$-CNOT circuits. Problem-specific designs, such as unitary coupled-cluster operators for quantum chemistry, warrant dedicated follow-up experiments.
    \item \textbf{Stationary Point vs. Global Basin Guarantees:} Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence} proves an ergodic $\mathcal{O}(1/T)$ rate to first-order stationary points ($\min_{1 \le t \le T} \|\nabla E(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1})\|_2^2 \le \epsilon$) under uniform margin conditions. Proving convergence to the global ground-state basin on non-convex, frustrated quantum manifolds remains an open theoretical challenge.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

SIGMA-QGD introduces a zero-extra-circuit, variance-aware curvature proxy that combines the advantages of first-order gradient methods and metric-aware quantum natural gradients. The algorithm extracts curvature information directly from the temporal statistics of parameter-shift evaluations, achieving consistent convergence gains across spin models and system sizes while staying strictly within the standard $2p+1$ circuit budget.

Future work will focus on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fusing the trajectory-variance proxy with exact analytic Hessian diagonals to capture both historical and instantaneous curvature signals;
    \item Validating performance on physical NISQ quantum processors subject to device noise;
    \item Extending the framework to chemistry Hamiltonians and unitary coupled-cluster (UCC) ans\"atze.
\end{itemize}
